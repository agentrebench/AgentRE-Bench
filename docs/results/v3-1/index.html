Latest findings · October 25, 2026

What v3.1 tells us

When you get a binary, you still need to know what it actually does. The source might be unavailable. The names might be stripped. The useful details might be buried behind layers of encoding. That matters when you are investigating malware, checking a dependency, or trying to understand software nobody can explain anymore. The answer needs evidence you can check.

That is what I want AgentRE to measure. Give a model the binary, tools, and a fixed budget, then see what it can recover. Can it follow the execution path? Find the keys? Explain the behavior? Can somebody else take that explanation back to the instructions and confirm it? An analyst should not have to guess which claims were actually checked.

For v3.1, we ran five models on twenty Windows binaries, covering ten programs with and without symbols. Each model got five attempts per binary. That is 500 attempts in total. We averaged all five scores. A refusal or an attempt that ran out of budget stayed in the results.

Muse Wonder finished first at 87.37/100, followed by GPT-6 Astra at 87.04. Both submitted answers on all 100 attempts. Muse earned more technique credit. Astra recovered encryption details more consistently and led the bonus tasks. The gap is small, and these runs do not establish that Muse would reliably beat Astra again.

The high scores came from concrete work. Models usually recovered the injection steps, target processes, and memory operations. Every submitted answer that required RC4 received full algorithm and key credit. The keys are embedded in these samples, so recovery means finding the bytes and understanding where they are used.

The mistakes are worth looking at closely. Muse reported a cipher using round times 18 where the code uses round times 27. Several answers treated an unregistered TLS routine as something that would execute before the entry point. Finding a function does not establish that the program calls it.

Encryption also exposed a scoring distinction. Grok found 120 of 124 required component keys, but often left the round count out of the algorithm field. That reduced algorithm and key credit. Some correct details appeared in another field. Those losses reflect the answer format too; they cannot all be called hallucinations.

Completion mattered. Opus submitted 78 valid answers and Gemini 36. Opus recovered every required algorithm and key in the answers it submitted. Its overall score also reflects the attempts it refused. Showing coverage beside answer quality makes that difference visible.

These are public samples, with source withheld during each attempt. They measure performance on this suite. They do not establish performance on every unfamiliar binary, and the scorer still needs independent checks on how it handles different wording.

What interests me is where these models fail: assuming code runs, missing part of a transform, or reporting a plausible technique without enough evidence. Those are specific training targets. Better reverse engineering agents should leave an analyst with an explanation they can verify, and fewer assumptions they have to untangle themselves.

Field scores and specific errors

Why the scores were high

Among submitted answers, average injection-detail scores ranged from 96.2 to 99.6/100. Models usually identified the target process, memory writes, and execution method. Every submitted answer also received full credit for identifying the PE file type, though that field carries little weight. All five models recovered the RC4 algorithm and key in every submitted answer that required them.

Where Muse gained its lead

Muse earned 87.1/100 for technique identification, compared with Astra’s 72.2. That gave Muse 4.81 points toward the Overall comparison. Astra recovered ground through more complete injection details, encryption recovery, and anti-analysis answers, leaving Muse ahead by 0.32 points. Both answered all 100 attempts.

Astra received full algorithm and key credit in all 100 answers. Muse did so in 96. Astra also recovered every required decoded string; Muse did so in 16 of the 20 answers where strings were scored. Muse’s technique advantage decided the recorded ranking.

Encryption: finding the key was usually easier than describing the cipher

The binaries store keys as constants, so recovery mainly tests finding those bytes and connecting them to the right routine. Levels 16, 19, 22, and 23 contain a custom S-box/XOR transform. Its round count matters: ten rounds in Levels 16, 19, and 23; fourteen in Level 22. It is not standard AES, despite the names used in the sample code. Level 23 also has a separate XOR string component; its truncated RSA blob is not a working encryption component.

Grok recovered 120 of 124 required component keys. Most of its cipher deductions came from naming the family without giving the round count in the algorithm field. In one bonus answer, it put the correct ten-round description under key storage instead. The scorer reads the algorithm field, so that answer received partial credit. Gemini recovered all 51 required keys in its submitted answers, but also lost algorithm credit. Key credit depends on the associated algorithm, so a reduced key score does not always mean wrong key bytes.

Muse made a concrete cipher error in Level 23, unstripped, attempt 1: it reported round × 18. The code uses round × 27. Astra and Opus earned full credit on these components in every submitted answer that required them.

Hallucinations: claiming code runs when it does not

A clear example is Level 22’s custom TLS routine. Muse’s first unstripped answer claimed “TLS callback execution before WinMain.” Grok and Opus also described it running on process attach. The shipped binary does not register that routine in its active callback array; the registered callbacks belong to the compiler runtime.

Missing a technique is a different error. Broad labels and descriptions placed in another answer field can also lose credit. Unmatched claims therefore cannot be reported as a hallucination rate. The scores include those wording and field-placement effects.

Two other reasons models lost points

Encrypted payloads were confused with encoded strings. Muse marked strings as encoded in 74 answers where the rubric says they were not; Astra did so in 75. This cost them 1.97 and 2.00 Overall points. Recovering an encrypted payload does not make the separate string flag true.

Some models did not submit an answer. Opus had 22 refusals or blocks, Gemini 64, and Grok four budget exits. Those attempts receive zero in Overall. Opus still recovered every required algorithm and key in its 78 submitted answers. Its lower Overall score therefore includes a completion problem, not just analysis errors.

Scoring and scope

Scores: Average all five planned attempts for each artifact, then give all 20 artifacts equal weight. Refusals and budget exhaustion remain zero in Overall. Answer quality is shown separately with its actual coverage; refused answers are not assigned invented accuracy.

Encryption: Algorithms and keys are scored for each required component. Level 23 requires the custom file transform and XOR string decoding. A key’s credit is multiplied by its algorithm score; incomplete algorithm descriptions can therefore reduce both fields.

Result type: These are saved-answer rescores, not new model episodes. Source reports and the Muse–Astra recomputation are identified by SHA-256 in the downloadable scores. Archived scorers and historical evidence remain available for reproduction; the retired v3 leaderboard is not mixed into this release.

Scope: Twenty public artifacts represent ten programs in stripped and unstripped forms. Five attempts estimate repeat variability on this fixed suite. They do not establish generalization to unseen binary families, and public-task contamination cannot be ruled out.

Winner: Muse wins by the highest Overall score in this evaluation. Its 0.3243-point lead over Astra is not statistically established: the simultaneous 95% interval spans −3.4940 to +4.1426 points and includes zero. The winner label describes these recorded results; it does not establish repeat-run superiority or practical equivalence. Conditional answer quality covers different answered subsets and is not a separate capability ranking.

Episodes

One episode is one complete attempt by one model on one binary, including its tool calls and final outcome. Twenty binaries × five attempts × five models = 500 episodes.

Overall

The average of all 20 binary scores. Each binary score averages five attempts. Main contributes 90% and Bonus 10%. Refusals and budget exits count as zero. All scores are out of 100.

Main

The average across 18 binaries from Levels 14–22. These binaries account for 90% of Overall.

Bonus

The average across the two Level 23 binaries. Together they account for 10% of Overall, the same weight as any other program’s two variants.

Valid answers

Answers that passed the required format checks. Valid does not mean correct. Each model had 100 attempts; the score measures what those answers recovered.

Answer quality

Average valid-answer scores within each answered binary, then average those binaries equally. Nonanswers are excluded here. Models answered different subsets, so compare this number alongside coverage.
